*Someone asked me what the next generation should be learning. I didn't have a clean answer, so I went looking for one in the last time this happened.*

---

## I. The wrong century

Everyone reaches for the same analogy, and most people reach for the wrong part of it.

The spinning jenny shows up, weavers smash it, and the lesson is supposed to be that the Luddites were wrong and everything turned out fine. It's a comforting story and it isn't useful, because the 1770s were the panic, not the transition. The machines had arrived. Nobody had yet worked out what to do about them.

The useful arc is **1890 to 1950** — the sixty years when society actually answered the question. And the answer was not what you'd expect.

## II. What they learned before

Two systems, barely touching each other.

For a thin layer at the top there was the classical curriculum: Latin, Greek, rhetoric, logic. Not vocational, never pretending to be, and essentially unchanged for three centuries.

For everyone else there was apprenticeship — and not as a figure of speech. The Statute of Artificers of 1563 made it English law that **nobody could practise a trade without serving seven years to a master.** Skill moved person to person, slowly, by doing, inside a relationship with obligations running both ways.

Look at what that actually was. A protected corridor from childhood to competence. You entered it knowing nothing and producing little, and someone was responsible for turning you into a practitioner. The knowledge lived in people, and time spent beside them was the only way to move it.

And for most of the rest of the curriculum — memorisation, penmanship, mental arithmetic, hand drafting — the whole design was to turn a farm kid into a shop clerk. **Everything valued was execution, accelerated by the human body.**

## III. What actually broke

In 1813, Parliament abolished the apprenticeship requirement.

Two hundred and fifty years of law, gone, swept away as a restraint on trade. The factories didn't want seven-year apprentices. They wanted operatives who could be trained in a fortnight.

So the machines didn't simply compete with skilled adults. **They removed the mechanism by which an unskilled child became a skilled adult,** and offered in its place a job that taught you almost nothing and led almost nowhere.

The Mechanics' Institutes started improvising a response in the 1820s, and they described the problem exactly. Industrialisation, they wrote, *"destroyed the inadequate old apprentice system and threw into relief the connection between material advancement and the necessity of education."*

They knew the ladder was gone. They had no idea yet what to put in its place.

## IV. How long it took, and that it was a choice

Here's the uncomfortable number. **The ladder broke in 1813. A replacement didn't reach most young people until 1940.**

State elementary schools arrived with the Forster Act in 1870. Schooling became compulsory in 1880, to age ten. In 1910, **18% of American 15-to-18-year-olds were enrolled in high school and 9% of eighteen-year-olds graduated.** By 1940 enrolment was **73%**, and the median young American had a diploma.

A hundred and twenty-seven years. Four or five generations lived in the gap, and they weren't lazy or unlucky — the institution that would have carried them did not exist yet.

But — and this is the part the fatalist version misses — **the rebuild wasn't weather. It was designed.** Dewey published *Democracy and Education* in 1916. The Smith–Hughes Act, federal money for vocational education, passed the Senate the same year. The manual training and agricultural education movements were deliberate answers to the machine age, argued over and paid for by people who could see what had happened.

**It was late and it was a choice.** Both halves matter. The first says this doesn't fix itself. The second says it can be fixed.

## V. The part everyone gets wrong

When education finally reformed, **it did not teach people to operate machines.**

There was no national curriculum in spinning-jenny maintenance. What spread instead was literacy and numeracy, and then a general secondary education that in the American case was deliberately *academic* rather than industrial.

That looks like a mistake. It was the opposite. By the time schooling caught up, the machines had turned over several times — anyone trained on the 1820 machine was obsolete by 1850. What survived every turnover was the general-purpose substrate: read an instruction, handle a number, follow an argument, and therefore learn whatever arrived next.

**The winning skill was never the machine. It was the capacity to be retrained by it.**

And the kids who won weren't the ones who learned to spin thread where the mule had taken over. They learned engineering, management, chemistry, accounting — **the systems around the machine.** The ones who funnelled into the office tower built the largest middle class in history, doing a job that would have sounded absurd in 1770. *You sit at a desk and push paper?*

The ladder didn't get repaired. **A different one got built next to it, and the next cohort climbed that one.**

## VI. The same shape, now

AI isn't mainly taking senior work. It's taking the bottom rungs.

The boilerplate function. The first-pass research memo. The diligence summary. The basic model. The intern's draft. Every one of those was economically marginal and pedagogically essential — **you did it badly, someone corrected you, and that was the apprenticeship.**

That corridor is closing the way it closed in 1813. Not because anyone decided to close it, but because the work a beginner used to do is now done faster by something that doesn't learn from doing it and never becomes senior.

So the honest statement of the problem isn't "AI takes jobs." It's that **the entry-level task was the training mechanism, and it's being automated before we've built its replacement.**

## VII. It's not that there's nothing to learn

This is the sentence I'd want a seventeen-year-old to hold onto:

**It's not that there's nothing to learn. It's that the specific thing to learn changed, and the old thing got cheaper.**

Maths and programming haven't become useless. They've become *commodities*. Someone who only does calculus, or only writes CRUD apps, is now competing with a machine that does it at near-zero marginal cost. That person loses.

But the person who asks *which thing should we compute, and what does the answer mean* — that person compounds. Machines are extraordinarily good at solving a stated problem and extraordinarily bad at the thing that moved humanity forward every previous time automation hit: **choosing what's worth doing, and what to do about it.**

## VIII. So why go to school at all

School was never really about the content. The content was the apparatus for building three things:

**Attention and grit.** The ability to work through hard, non-obvious problems for years. Automation doesn't grant this. It removes the incentive to develop it, if you never built it young.

**Thinking under constraint.** Learning to think *without* a machine beside you — so you know what you're delegating, and so you can smell when the machine is wrong. You need an unassisted baseline or you have nothing to calibrate against.

**Domain judgment.** The machine can run ten thousand legal reviews. It cannot know which contract matters and why the client cares. That knowing takes the messiest, least automatable thing there is: years inside a domain, developing taste.

And there's a practical point underneath the philosophical one. **College today bundles two products** — a credential, which is a signal to employers, and an apprenticeship, which is four years of supervised correction by people better than you. The credential is depreciating fast. The apprenticeship is getting more valuable, and nobody markets it.

Information was never the scarce good; it's less scarce now than at any moment in history. The scarce good is **someone who will tell you precisely why your work is wrong, repeatedly, for years.** Choose the school, the department, and above all the people on that basis alone.

## IX. What I'd actually tell them to study

**The timeless — these are the moat now:**

**Reading and writing at a high level.** The ability to think in long-form, structured argument. This is first because agents produce text but can't decide what's true or worth saying. Whoever can direct and judge prose owns the output channel.

**Systems thinking.** The physics of how large things fail — supply chains, incentives, markets, organisations. *"Why did this collapse"* is the skill.

**Statistics and reasoning under uncertainty.** Not the mechanics, which machines nail. The epistemics: what do we actually know, what's the base rate, when is evidence good enough to act on.

**One human domain, deeply.** Law, medicine, biology, markets, history, a craft. Deep enough that you have taste in it. **Taste is incompressible.**

**The specific — what's scarce in the agent economy right now:**

**How to build and manage teams of agents.** Orchestration, evaluation, trust-tiering, cost-per-outcome. This is management, and it's a real, learnable skill today.

**How agents transact.** Identity, reputation, escrow, verifiable output, payments at the protocol layer. The rails.

**How to prompt-evaluate, not prompt-ask.** The difference between typing a question and building an eval harness that tells you when the output is right. That's the new engineering craft.

**Systems of trust.** When to trust machine output, who is accountable when it's wrong, and how incentive structures stop the machine gaming you.

## X. What the job actually looks like

The honest prediction: **mostly the same job, except you run a fleet instead of doing the work.**

The lawyer runs ten thousand cases through agent teams, owns the client relationship, and sets the strategy. The analyst directs an autonomous portfolio, owns the thesis, and answers for it. The engineer specifies what to build, evaluates whether it's correct, and owns the outcome to a human user.

**Execution moves to the agents. Accountability, judgment and taste stay human.** Optimise for being the person who understands the domain well enough to trust nothing the machine says. That's the un-automatable core.

And the roles that don't exist yet will have names nobody has heard. They always do. Nobody in 1890 could have written "electrical engineer" as a career path.

## XI. The uncomfortable part

The machines won't replace all of us. **They'll replace the people who only learned the thing the machine now does cheaper.**

What you get paid for is being exactly one level up the stack from whatever just got commoditised. And that level keeps climbing. Which means the real skill isn't a subject at all — it's **becoming comfortable with perpetual obsolescence and relearning.**

That's the thing to teach a kid. Not a fixed curriculum. The expectation that their job is to keep moving up as the floor comes up underneath them.

## XII. And the thing worth building

The historical answer to "the ladder broke" wasn't a retraining scheme. It was a **new institution** — and it took a century to arrive.

We should be able to do it faster. The gap between the apprenticeship dying and its replacement existing is exactly where the next great institution gets built. It won't look like a university and it won't look like a bootcamp. It'll look like **paid, supervised, real work, early, with someone accountable for making you good** — an apprenticeship rebuilt for a world where the machine writes the first draft and the human is trained on the correction.

That's a company, not a policy. Whoever builds it will matter more than most of what gets funded this decade.

---

**The short version, if someone asks you at a party:**

Execution is becoming free and judgment is not. The machines will do the work. They won't decide what's worth doing, they can't tell you when they're confidently wrong, and they cannot be held responsible for the answer. Learn the things that compound, go deep enough in one real domain to have taste, and pick the place that will correct you hardest.

The jobs will have names you haven't heard yet. They always do.
